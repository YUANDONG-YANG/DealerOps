The Saturday peak workload mix does not pass, and the graph shows that one transaction group is responsible rather than the system as a whole. Catalog search is the only outright failure. Its curve has already turned by 100 transactions — by far the steepest climb of the three — it breaches its 10 s maximum at roughly 173 transactions, before reaching the 200-transaction peak it must sustain.

Purchase steps passes with real margin (5.2 s against a 7 s limit at 500 transactions, no knee in range), so it has room to absorb growth. Logon/Log off technically passes but is the hidden risk: flat at ~2.2 s to 400 transactions, then a visible step to 3.0 s, where it sits exactly on its limit with zero headroom. A flat line that steps up once and then stops rising usually means a saturated fixed-size resource (session, connection or authentication pool), so any load beyond 500 transactions would be expected to push it over.

Part 3 — Reflection

1. How does graphing the data help more than reading the table?

Performance requirements are about shape, and the graph shows shape. Plotted against the requirement lines, pass/fail is read in a second — a solid line above its own dashed line fails — instead of eleven comparisons per column. The graph also exposes what the table hides, the rate of change: catalog search’s early knee and logon’s step at 400–450 appear as slope, and interpolating gives the crossing point (~173 transactions) that is in no row of the data. On one scale it is also obvious that the three groups degrade differently, so the problem is one component, not the application.

2. What should the development team do next?

Make catalog search the single priority and profile it under load to find where the time goes — query plans and missing indexes, result-set size and paging, cold or missing caching, pool contention. The curve turning inside the first 100 transactions points at work that grows with load, so moving to bigger hardware is the wrong first move. Then re-run the same workload mix and re-plot it to confirm catalog search holds under 10 s through 200 transactions without shifting cost onto the other groups. Separately, raise logon/log off as a risk: find what saturates at 400–450, since it meets its requirement by 0.0 s. Purchase steps needs no work and serves as the regression baseline.

3. How would component-level performance testing differ?

The mix answers “does the system meet its requirements on a realistic Saturday?”; component testing answers “which part is slow, and why?” Each transaction type would be driven in isolation against one component — search service, endpoint, database query — with the other traffic removed, so there is no contention between groups. Measurement shifts from one end-to-end time per group to per-layer timings and resource counters (CPU, I/O, query time, pool use), charted per component against concurrency, and the user-facing 3/7/10 s budgets would be split per component. The trade-off: component results point straight at the cause but can look healthy while the mix still fails, because isolation removes the very contention the Saturday mix creates.
